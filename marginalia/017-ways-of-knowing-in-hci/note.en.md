# Ways of Knowing in HCI — reading note: eighteen ways of knowing and the accountabilities that govern them

> A reading note on the methods handbook. 18 chapters + prologue/epilogue, 34 leading authors, Springer 2014. This is not a book about *how to do research*; it is a book about *how to judge whether research is any good*. Every method is made to answer the same four questions (essence / intellectual history / what it can answer / what constitutes good work), and the book's real theoretical claim hides in Gaver's chapter: **science and design answer to different forms of accountability (epistemological vs. aesthetic); review a paper under the wrong accountability framework and you will miss the insight it actually contains.**

## 1. What kind of book this is

The origin is clear: at the 2010 HCIC (Human Computer Interaction Consortium) meeting, a group of senior HCI researchers realized they could not competently review methods they had never been trained in. They ran tutorials for two years, then turned them into this book. Editors: Judith S. Olson (Donald Bren Professor at UC Irvine) and Wendy A. Kellogg (founder of the Social Computing Group at IBM Watson Research).

The disciplinary background from the prologue: HCI grew out of the cognitive-psychology human-factors tradition of Card, Moran & Newell's 1983 *The Psychology of Human-Computer Interaction*, dominated by experiments and surveys; Suchman's 1987 *Plans and Situated Actions* brought in ethnography; then design arrived as a way of knowing, alongside technology researchers building new artifacts and toolkits. As methods multiplied, reviewers and readers began asking: **"What counts as good research? How do we know whether the advance is done well? Can we trust the findings? Do we know more now than we did?"** This book is the collected answer to that question.

The book's posture is self-aware:

- **Written for readers, not practitioners.** Chapter 1 is Paul Dourish on *how to read ethnography* — "this chapter does not set out to instruct the reader on conducting ethnographic research……It is to explain how to read, interpret, and understand ethnographic work" (p. 1). The goal is not to train experts but "able reviewers of a variety of methods well beyond those we were formally trained in."
- **A uniform four-part template**: essence of the method → history and intellectual tradition → what questions it can and cannot answer → how to do it / what constitutes good work; plus three recurring extras: a curated list of references for becoming expert, exemplar papers, and the author's personal story (why they fell for this craft). Every chapter ends with exercises — the self-discipline of a textbook.
- **The editors admit the coverage is incomplete**: no chapters on interviewing (Kvale), Contextual Inquiry (Beyer & Holtzblatt), cognitive modeling (the Card-Moran-Newell lineage), or historical research. The epilogue puts it beautifully: thirty years into the discipline, one volume cannot hold all its ways of knowing — "That could be a really good sign of the health and vitality of the field, or a clear harbinger of intellectual chaos. We think it is a good sign."

## 3. Four axes that run through the book

### Axis 1: Is data "picked up" or "generated"?

One pole is Dourish quoting Marilyn Strathern: ethnography is "the deliberate attempt to generate more data than the investigator is aware of at the time of collection" (p. 2) — data is the product of participation; analytic protocols may be devised after the fact. This runs exactly counter to the define–measure–record–analyze–report loop.

The other pole is the shared creed of the three digital-trace chapters: logs "capture actual user behavior and not recalled behaviors or subjective impressions" (p. 350); the sensor chapter's tenet is "the results of a sensor data stream study can be no better than the sensed data" (p. 300); eye tracking offers a physiological proxy for attention.

But both poles self-interrogate: the log chapter's axiom is that "raw data cannot be assumed to correctly and completely represent the data being recorded" (p. 366); Gaver's Cultural Probes deliberately treat unscientificness as a design resource — participants are told they "should feel free to tell us stories—or simply lie" (p. 156). And the retrospective chapter (Russell & Chi) performs the book's most elegant epistemic maneuver: abandoning veridicalism, **memory itself is the data** — "The veridicality of reconstructed experiences can be of minimal importance as these memories will guide future behavior" (p. 386).

### Axis 2: Accountability — what each method must answer for, and to whom

Gaver's chapter is the book's theoretical core. Science lives under **epistemological accountability**: you must explain and defend how you know what you claim is true; the method is part of the result. Design lives under **aesthetic accountability**: you must show that it *works* — functionally, socially, culturally, aesthetically; "aesthetic" is about the composition's satisfactoriness, not prettiness. "In design, even the most meticulous methodology will not redeem a bad design, and even the most hare-brained processes will not ruin a good one." (p. 147)

Gaver cuts two levels deeper:

- Via Latour: the canonical "scientific method" is a post-hoc rationalization — "a lie by omission not commission, and a fiction that guides and constrains real scientific activity" (p. 150). In his opening he confesses that when he wrote up his impact-sound experiments, all the shopping for wood, mount improvisation, and analysis-searching vanished from the paper, because they did not fit the causal flow the writeup needed to establish.
- The hypothesis/design-proposal analogy looks natural but is "positively dangerous": hypotheses project from theoretically held truths, proposals are "tools for imagining things that do not yet exist"; experiments hold factors apart, designs arrange configurations where elements "merge and blend like ingredients in a recipe."

In the epilogue, Kellogg & Olson widen Gaver's dichotomy into a spectrum: experiments/surveys build credibility by eliminating alternative explanations; GTM builds abstractions "accountable to the data"; ethnography and action research build credibility by "characterizing and disclosing the qualities of the engagement and of the researcher as the instrument of knowing"; design and system building must show that they work. Then comes the book's most important methodological warning (p. 470): **"If we misunderstand the way of knowing employed by a study, or apply to it an inappropriate accountability framework, we risk missing the insights that may be contained within it."**

Hudson & Mankoff supply the invention-side fine print: an invention's value belongs to the concept itself — "it might be that the concept came to them in a dream the night before. However, if both paths lead to the same invention, it is equally good" (p. 73); validation runs through proof-of-concept: "you do not believe it until it has been built" (p. 78); expert "Wow, cool" prima facie judgment would be unacceptable in discovery work but is acceptable in invention work, where its uncertainty gets absorbed by follow-on work.

### Axis 3: Invention vs. discovery — and "the thing proceeds theory"

Hudson & Mankoff: discovery advances by refining "framing truths" (Fitts' law → microstructure-of-movement theories); invention advances by combining known parts into wholes that are more capable but less understood — "our understanding can sometimes actually decrease because we are creating more complex things that are less well understood than the simpler things they are made of" (p. 72). They also name the field's structural bias: we hear "Where's the science in this?" and "That's just engineering," almost never the reverse.

Zimmerman & Forlizzi quote Carroll & Kellogg 1989 on HCI's oldest ache: "the thing proceeds theory instead of theory driving the creation of new things" (p. 168) — the mouse was invented before studies showed it was a good design. RtD's ambition is to let new things enter the world, spawn theory, and keep a running dialogue with existing theory.

### Axis 4: At what level does generalization happen?

The epilogue asks: if good work does not all aim at theory, how does HCI accumulate knowledge? The chapters form a spectrum of answers:

- **Statistical generalization**: surveys (probability sampling + Groves' total error chain: coverage / sampling / measurement / non-response), experiments (randomization + power analysis).
- **Corpus juxtaposition**: Dourish — "The level of ethnographic generalization then is often the corpus, rather than the specific study" (p. 13); asking about a single study's "representativeness" is a category error.
- **Transferability + workability**: Hayes's action research trades generalizability for transferability, with the final arbiter being whether the solution works for the community partner.
- **Theoretical saturation**: Muller's GTM uses fit / relevance / work / modifiability (Glaser & Strauss) and credibility / resonance / originality / usefulness (Charmaz), with "no further surprises" as the stopping rule.
- **Annotated portfolios**: Gaver — design knowledge is most credible up close: "Design knowledge is most trustworthy when it stays close to designed artefacts" (p. 162); abstraction dilutes it.
- **Contingent conclusions**: Ren & Kraut's ABM reproduces stylized facts before extrapolating, and its conclusions come out contingent: "There is no universal optimal design for all online communities" (p. 414).

## 4. Group-by-group reading

### The qualitative trio: the researcher is the instrument

**Dourish (how to read)**: two definitions — Strathern above; Sherry Ortner: "the attempt to understand another life world using the self—or as much of it as possible—as the instrument of knowing" (p. 2). Five historical movements: 1910s Malinowski, stranded in the Trobriands by WWI, turns participant-observation into anthropology's defining method; 1920s the Chicago School applies it to urban subcultures; 1960s structuralism (Lévi-Strauss: meaning arises through patterns of difference; the object of analysis shifts from event to system of events); 1970s the hermeneutic turn (Geertz: culture as "webs of significance," ethnography moves from explanation to interpretation — thick description; culture shifts from a "taxonomic" to a "generative" view); 1980s reflexivity (*Writing Culture*: notice sentences like "The Nuer trade in cattle" — definitive, eternal ethnographic present, author vanished); 1990s multi-sited ethnography (the field dissolves; follow people and objects).

His reviewer's checklist comes in two columns. Good questions: What are the work's empirical claims? What are its **conceptual** claims (a different question!)? What was the context of production? How does it contribute to the corpus? Bad questions: "Is this a representative sample?" (ethnographic data does not stand for a statistical phenomenon); "How can you tell if what people told you is right?" — "A lie is revealing; it suggests that there is something worth lying about" (p. 18); ask rather "what warrants that answer?"; "Didn't you affect things by being there?" — "I should hope so" (p. 18); and "What should I build now that I know this?" — implications for design do not sit inside the ethnographic text but in how it reframes design's questions. He also devotes a passage to ethnography ≠ ethnomethodology: Suchman 1987 is EM but not ethnographic; her 1983/1993 accountant and airport studies are both.

**Muller (grounded theory)**: GTM is a family of methods, not one method, suited to domains without a dominant theory; its core is institutionalizing abductive inference. Quoting Peirce: "Deduction proves that something must be; Induction shows that something actually is operative; Abduction… suggests that something may be." (p. 28) The chapter's sharpest line is the saturation criterion: "Surprise told us where to go next. Lack of surprise told us we were done." (p. 39) The intellectual history is a civil war: Glaser (theory emerges) vs. Strauss/Corbin (procedural) vs. Charmaz (constructivist)/Clarke (situational analysis). Quality: test a theory at its weakest point; aim for desirable failures. Of GTM's three usage patterns in HCI, the most suspect is "using GTM to signal a deep and iterative coding approach" without giving coding detail — the work cannot be picked up or entered into the literature.

**Hayes (action research)**: Lewin's lineage ("nothing is as practical as a good theory"), pragmatist roots. "The cornerstone of AR is that these two cannot be disentangled: the doing and the knowing, the intervention and the learning." (p. 49) The first duty is always to the community partner — even data "of little to no interest to the HCI research community" (p. 59) must enter the analysis. The risk is *model monopoly*: the professional researcher's model dominating the conversation. Two counterintuitive details: **celebration as method** — gift bags and parties for teachers, because public celebration "can be much more effective in building good will and compensating research participants for involvement than simple cash payments" (p. 61); and the exit paradox — successful AR produces sustainable change, and once change sustains itself it "can be less interesting from a research standpoint" (p. 62). She entered AR through an IRB meeting: the federal definition requires research to produce generalizable knowledge, while AR aims precisely at local change — the method and the ethics regime collide by definition.

### The building trio: systems as instruments of inquiry

**Hudson & Mankoff (technical research)**: contributions split into direct creation vs. enabling research (tools / systems / basic capabilities / importing-and-adapting — Newman's 1968 introduction of finite-state machines into UI implementation counts as a research contribution). Concept sources: needs-first vs. technology-first; the latter has a bad reputation in HCI, but they defend it — technology changes fast, needs change slowly, and MEMS accelerometers traveled from airbags to phone orientation-switching to prove it. The robustness ladder of validation: prose → screenshots → video → live demo → user testing → deployment — later rungs are more credible but yield diminishing returns, and deployment can inject confounds alien to the core invention (their sound-to-text system for deaf users had 9-hour latencies and a single transcriber; "all of these barriers were peripheral to the invention itself," p. 80). Tools are validated by threshold / ceiling / breadth of coverage (Myers, Hudson & Pausch 2000): "performing a usability test on an example built with a tool would likely tell us almost nothing about the tool" (p. 88). The closing confession: these judgment calls are transmitted by osmosis from mentor to student; almost no course teaches them — "This chapter has set out to rectify some of those gaps by putting common practice, and the rationale behind it, into words."

**Terveen et al. (online communities)**: access to real communities comes in four levels — usage data, users, APIs, infrastructure; "as one ascends the levels, more powerful methods can be deployed to answer research questions in more authentic contexts" (p. 96). Hence study, build, repeat: GroupLens/MovieLens/Cyclopath as self-built living laboratories — Minnesota employed a full-time engineer for over a decade at a cumulative cost above one million dollars; the biggest risk is that nobody comes, and such failures are "rather boring: you did not pick a problem that people really cared about" (p. 99). Wikipedia's bitter lesson: editors resent being treated as guinea pigs ("Wikipedia editors were extremely averse to being treated as 'guinea pigs,'" p. 102); auto-posting SuggestBot recommendations met fierce resistance, and a recruitment study nearly got team members banned. The loveliest finding: deliberately showing users wrong predicted ratings moved their own ratings toward the error while satisfaction dropped — the system can manipulate users, and users have metacognition about it.

**Siek et al. (field deployments)**: deployment = a field study that trials a newly created technology (usually a prototype) in situ, in three forms: convenience (eating your own dog food, with acquiescence bias — a friend proudly reported "logging out" of an app whose version had no logout button), semi-controlled, and in the wild. Core claim: "attracting voluntary adoption and use is an extraordinarily high bar for any technology to meet" (p. 126); research questions may evolve mid-deployment; researcher closeness is not contamination but insight — "these relationships can provide insights and additional research questions that may broaden the research contribution" (p. 127). The four personal testimonials each have a character: Katie's double question ("But can they do that?!" then, after the lab study, "Sure, but can they do that all day long?"), Gillian's Lewin credo, Mark's pain-vs-knowledge calculus, John's positioning of deployment "at the intersection of designing something new and ethnographically observing it in use." Counterintuitive details: a 24-hour hotline with zero calls did not mean no problems — participants were afraid to dial a number with a foreign area code; some participants, to avoid keeping diaries, stopped greeting colleagues — the act of measurement changed the behavior being measured.

### The design pair: from accountability to reframing

**Gaver**: see Axis 2. On practice: projects run context → proposals (~50 per project, collected into workbooks so a design space can emerge) → refinement (consensus arrives with "a kind of 'audible click'") → assessment across many voices — besides ethnographic observation, they commission independent documentary filmmakers (not told the design intent), hire poets to write about the devices, invite journalists to write independently, because the multiple readings of the Local Barometers (poetic objects / subliminal advertising / gifts / cutting-edge design / broken electronics) "may all be equally true," and merging them by force would only "flatten the experiences afforded by the designs" (p. 160). Warning: imposing methodological frameworks (brainstorming, personas, probes) to tame design's uncertainty also forfeits the innovative dividends of productive indiscipline.

**Zimmerman & Forlizzi**: RtD's three lineages (Koskinen et al. 2011's Lab/Field/Showroom: the Dutch rich-interaction laboratory tradition, Scandinavian participatory design, British critical design). Frayling's 1993 into/for/through triad. Their own CHI 2007 model: RtD integrates three knowledge inputs — *how* (from engineers), *true* (behavioral scientists), *real* (anthropologists) — and yields four kinds of research output: technical opportunities, gaps in behavioral theory, new practices, and design patterns. The theory-gap example is lovely: product-attachment theory explains why parents cherish the picture books they read to their children but fails to explain why the attachment fails to form when the same stories are read on an eReader. The Reverse Alarm Clock case is a complete demonstration of **reframing**: from "help parents with children waking at night" → redefined from the child's view as a usability problem (children have no sense of duration; parents are inconsistent) → "support parents in parenting rather than automate parenting" → finally "part of a celebrated life-stage transition" — home technology should be introduced at transitions, not in reaction to family breakdown. Snackbot shows how prototyping a future state produces knowledge: the snack-delivery robot triggered workplace ripple effects — politeness, protection, mimicry, social comparison, even jealousy.

Read together, the pair is instructive: Gaver supplies RtD's epistemological defense (design is not science's poor cousin), Zimmerman & Forlizzi supply its institutional manual (how CHI gradually absorbed RtD — two design-research paper committees today).

### The scale quartet: cheap methods are the most abused

**Experiments (Gergle & Tan)**: the Neyman–Pearson tradition; hypotheses must be precise / meaningful / testable / falsifiable; crossover interactions can render main effects invisible (averaging over the other factor shows "no difference" while opposite-signed interactions cancel); the cost of factorial explosion: 3×3×4×2 = 72 conditions, 720 participants at 10 per group in a between-subjects design. Reporting follows Abelson's **MAGIC** (Magnitude / Articulation / Generality / Interestingness / Credibility), with repeated hammering on p-value misreading: "a better (i.e., smaller) p value does not mean it is a 'more significant' or 'bigger' effect" (p. 215). The opening already diagnoses the replication crisis: "A poorly executed experiment may have the veneer of 'scientific validity' because of the methodological rigor, but ultimately provides little more than well-measured noise" (p. 195), and replication is "often less valued (and thus harder to publish) in HCI than the novelty of invention."

**Surveys (Müller et al.)**: from Galton's 1876 first psychological questionnaire, through Neyman establishing probability sampling as the gold standard, to Groves' total error framework. The operational checklist is dense: constructs before questions; coverage error in the sampling frame; 384 respondents for a large population at 95%/5%; five families of bias (satisficing / acquiescence / social desirability / response order / question order); hypothetical questions "should be entirely excluded" (p. 242). The most subversive finding: Groves' 2006 meta-analysis found "no meaningful correlation" between response rates and non-response error (p. 253) — the traditional wisdom of maximizing response rates is overthrown; the quality focus moves to assessing and adjusting for non-response bias. And a standing refrain: self-reported behavior is never as accurate as logs ("will always be more accurate," p. 233), but logs cannot say whether users were satisfied — complement, not substitute.

**Crowdsourcing (Egelman, Chi & Dow)**: a beginner tutorial plus a meditation on pay ethics. Rule of thumb: pay "relatively close to the prevailing minimum wage" — a loaded term for a global workforce; Dow is blunter: "we cannot be satisfied with paying workers $2–3 per hour on average" (p. 284). Quality control: gold-standard questions, open text boxes (raising forgery costs while making nonsense legible), reputation thresholds. The two sharpest observations: **price is sampling** — raising pay from $0.01 to $1.00 visibly rewrites the national composition of the sample; and the worker profile — 95% of respondents had white-collar jobs and answered at midday: crowdworkers are not professional subjects but people sneaking tasks in between work tasks. "Prior to crowdsourcing, the thought that a researcher could recruit over 1,000 subjects in under a week for under $1,000 was unheard of, but this is the new reality." (p. 286)

**Logs (Dumais, Jeffries, Russell, Tang & Teevan — an all-industry author list)**: a 2×3 frame: observational vs. experimental, across lab / field / log. Observational log analysis's three defenses (veracity, scale, no observer effect), but "Observational log analysis can only reveal what people do with the tools they have" (p. 356) — that Twitter cannot search old tweets does not mean nobody wants to. Experimental log studies (A/B tests) demand discipline: concurrent conditions, random assignment (never opt-in), uniform diversion, and **logging counterfactual events** — at a 1% trigger rate, failing to log counterfactuals inflates the sample needed to detect the same effect by three orders of magnitude (525K → 525M, p. 362). Cleaning axioms, the AOL and Netflix de-anonymization cases, and "statistical significance does not imply substantive significance" (undo usage rising from 0.1% to 0.12% is statistically reliable but perhaps unimportant, p. 364). The chapter closes with a confession: this craft has almost no textbooks — even power analysis links to Wikipedia; the chapter itself is an attempt to make corporate internal knowledge public.

The quartet's shared undertone: **when a method becomes cheap enough to use casually, discipline becomes the scarce resource**. The experiment chapter polices p-values; the survey chapter polices bias; the crowdsourcing chapter polices pay and quality; the log chapter polices cleaning and counterfactuals — each is hedging "easy" against "careless."

### The trace trio, plus memory

**Sensors (Voida, Patterson & Patel)**: three units of analysis (egocentric / group-centric / space-centric) plus IMS (infrastructure-mediated sensing through power lines, plumbing, HVAC). The hard boundary: "sensor data generally does a poor job of answering questions of why" (p. 299). The design red line: requiring participants to "dramatize" their routines so the sensors can detect them ("walking through a particular area of a room to indicate presence") destroys exactly the ecological validity this method buys. The machine-learning warning is eminently practical: cross-validation leakage can be invisible (temperatures stored as differences from a running mean correlate train and test folds through the shared mean; the classifier dazzles on paper and collapses in the field, p. 310). Their field story ends gently: "It turned out that the sensing did not have to be perfect." (p. 314) — participants reconstructed their days from the visualizations, which loops straight back to retrospective methods.

**Eye tracking (Navalpakkam & Churchill)**: the anatomical starting point — cone cells are only 6% of photoreceptors; eye movements are a sampling mechanism compensating for low peripheral resolution. All interpretive power rests on the eye-mind assumption (fixation = processing), so good work means explicitly checking its premises. Yarbus 1967 is the methodological anchor: the same painting inspected for "ages of the people" yields different scanpaths than for "material circumstances of the family" — **eye-tracking data is behavior under a task, not an objective reading of the stimulus**. The counterintuitive finding: "more time on page is not always good" (p. 341) — longer dwell under high distraction marks reading struggle; gaze patterns can predict subjective frustration with high accuracy. Market research answers whether users look at banner ads in three words: "(they do not)" (p. 335).

**Retrospective (Russell & Chi)**: RCR (retrospective cue-recall) — let behavior happen naturally, then reconstruct it afterward from cues captured at the time (full-screen screenshots, video). Five design dimensions; three time spans, each with its own pathology (short spans tempt the participant to tell "more than they can know"; long spans invite a tendency to "clean up their act"). Cue quality decides everything: query-string-plus-date cues are "effectively useless"; widening capture from the browser window to the whole screen lets even a corner of Excel in the background give "a distinct sense of time, activity, and place" (p. 384). Accuracy: ~86% correct predictions for events two days old, still 75% at seven days. And the memory need not be true — **memory is the substrate of future behavior**.

**The modeling/structure pair**: Ren & Kraut's ABM starts from what makes multiuser systems special — "the experience of some users is partly the result of decisions that earlier users have made" (p. 395), hence "interactive design and evaluation cycle, perhaps the most successful HCI technique for system design, is insufficient for the design of multiuser applications" (p. 395). ABM's value lies not in predictive precision but in **integrating multiple theories**: the same variable, group size, is positive under resource theories, negative under responsibility dilution (collective effort model), negative under interpersonal-bond theories — no single empirical study can adjudicate, but ABM folds the contending theories into one runnable model. A seven-step roadmap (appropriateness → conceptual model → computational translation → implementation → validation → virtual experiments → publication; the publication bar: "experienced modelers should be able to draft the pseudo code of the program based on the model description"). The case findings directly challenge received wisdom: a model calibrated on 12 Usenet groups and validated on 25 shows that broad-topic communities have more engaged members (more frequent logins) but not more posting; community-level deletion moderation increases commitment without increasing contribution. Hansen & Smith's SNA chapter is the textbook-style one: relational vs. attribute data — "more about 'who you know' than 'what you know' or 'who you are'" (p. 421); trees (node metrics) / forests (aggregate metrics) / thickets (clusters and motifs). The most beautiful result: "answer persons" can be predicted from purely structural features (high out-degree, disproportionate ties to isolates), reaching R²=.72 against content-analyst-identified roles — **structure predicts semantic roles**. The bad-work portrait is equally clear: publishing a network visualization without statistical validation — "Just as a mean does not provide any details about the distribution that generated it, a graph density metric does not provide any details about the network that generated it." (p. 437)

### The meta-layer: ethics (Bruckman)

From the Nuremberg Code (1949) through Tuskegee to the Belmont Report (1979) — she singles out justice as the most neglected of the three principles. Beyond institutional history are the internet's new dilemmas, with the most counterintuitive evidence coming from Hudson & Bruckman's chat-room experiment: consent requests were ignored, and "the researcher was kicked out of the chatroom in most cases because the process of requesting participation annoyed members" (p. 463) — requesting consent was more disruptive than the research. Another institutional absurdity: analyzing public online data is not human-subjects research, but asking its author a single question makes it one — so many researchers choose not to ask, producing "maddeningly incomplete papers" (p. 459). Anonymity cuts both ways: a single quoted post can be de-anonymized by Googling; and when participants want to be named, anonymization itself can cause harm. Her stance is calm and clear: "Laws shift at political boundaries, policies shift at institutional boundaries, and ethics shift at cultural boundaries." (p. 450); "The ethics of a study and the research value of a study are inseparable concepts." (p. 451) — ethics is not a compliance layer above method but a judgment problem isomorphic to every way of knowing in the book.

## 5. Reception and related research

- **Adopted as PhD reading on publication.** Erik Stolterman's May-2014 book note is representative: most methods are covered in more depth elsewhere; this book's uniqueness is that "all chapters are written by HCI researchers who can relate each method to HCI research," and "this book is already a required reading for all PhD students in the field." He also called for the community to debate the book — "In the name of developing a discipline we need all members of the HCI research community to engage in a book like this."
- **ACM Computing Reviews** (M. Sasikumar, December 2015): "an impressive collection in terms of the level of detail and variety"; "useful for any kind of research involving users and their experience."
- **Citation shape**: Crossref records ~146 formal citations to the book DOI, but book citations are chronically undercounted — the real influence runs through **chapter-level citation**: Muller's GTM chapter, Gaver's accountability chapter, and Zimmerman & Forlizzi's RtD chapter are standard citations in CSCW/DIS/CHI literatures; the chapters interlock with their authors' papers (Gaver 2012 CHI "What should we expect from research through design?"; Zimmerman et al. 2007 CHI; Koskinen et al. 2011 *Design Research through Practice*).
- **Lineage position**: the prologue and epilogue themselves name the "future ways of knowing" then emerging — third-paradigm HCI (Harrison, Sengers & Tatar 2011) and feminist HCI (Bardzell 2010/2011). The book is thus a snapshot of the transition from "second-paradigm cognitive psychology" to "post-third-paradigm methodological pluralism."
- **Editors and dedication**: Judith Olson is Donald Bren Professor at UC Irvine (the HCIC community grew up around Irvine); Wendy Kellogg founded IBM's Social Computing Group (1998), co-created "social translucence" with Thomas Erickson, and is an ACM Fellow and CHI Academy member. The book memorializes chapter-5 author John Riedl (father of GroupLens/MovieLens, died July 15, 2013).
- **2014 → 2026 in hindsight**: the book pre-figures the decade's anxieties — the replication crisis (experiments chapter: replication harder to publish), platform-data ethics (the log chapter's AOL/Netflix cases, Bruckman's ToS chapter), sensing and consent (the sensor chapter). What it could not foresee is the LLM becoming both research instrument and research subject — the 2014 "crowdsourcing" chapter reads today as the prehistory of the "human data" problem.

## 6. Connections to my own ongoing threads

1. **Entries 002 (Dourish's style) / 013 (ethnomethodology)**: chapter 1 *is* Dourish, and it is the clearest statement of the EM/ethnography distinction (Suchman 1987 is EM but not ethnographic). More importantly, "corpus-level generalization" — ethnography generalizes across the corpus, not within a single study — is the formal epistemological warrant for exactly how these marginalia are produced: read each text closely, juxtapose across texts.
2. **Entry 015 (UCI's qualitative landscape)**: Olson is UCI's Donald Bren Professor and HCIC is an Irvine-rooted community; this book is in a sense the UCI–IBM axis taking stock of the whole discipline — it slots into 015's UCI context.
3. **Entry 004 (quantifying the CHI/ACL "storytelling" problem)**: the book supplies a ready-made analytical coordinate — before arguing whether a paper is good, ask which accountability regime it invokes; the "storytelling" charge can be sharpened into "reviewing invention-style work by discovery-style standards, or vice versa."
4. **Entry 011 (auditable agents) / the SE×AI agenda**: AI-agent papers likewise straddle invention (the system works) and discovery (why it works); Hudson & Mankoff's triad (concept creation / proof-of-concept / other validation) works directly as a review rubric, and their tolerance for prima facie "Wow, cool" in invention work is a strong dose for debates over agent-demo papers.
5. **Muller's abduction chapter meets the corpus-first habit**: abduction ("something may be") plus surprise as the continue/stop signal is the most formal legitimation available for non-deductive reading — it upgrades "read the corpus first, search literature later" from personal preference to methodology.

## 7. How to read it

- The editors' advice is right: read it through, especially the methods you don't know — "The contrasts that emerge are fascinating and thought-provoking." (Epilogue)
- If only three chapters: Gaver (accountability), Dourish (how to read qualitative work), Hudson & Mankoff (invention and discovery). Those three are the book's theoretical skeleton; the other fifteen are muscle on that skeleton.
- Use the end-of-chapter Exercises as a check: all 18 chapters carry exercises — the self-discipline of a graduate textbook.
- The line worth pinning above the desk (p. 470): **"If we misunderstand the way of knowing employed by a study, or apply to it an inappropriate accountability framework, we risk missing the insights that may be contained within it."**
