> Every flagship model launch summons the same ritual: a screen recording, one line of prompt, a caption reading "one-shotted." The half-life of this excitement has compressed to days, while launches themselves now arrive weekly — and the endless procession of spectacles produces its own opposite: **aesthetic fatigue**. This essay connects Debord's notion of the spectacle to AI launch culture and treats the launch as a measurable temporal object: how long a spectacle lives, how its production is organized, how "fatigue" gets verbalized, and the double temporality — AGI utopia versus earnings anxiety — behind the whole discourse. Two postscripts draw on Andrew Abbott and Douglas Adams.

## 1. A shrinking spectacle cycle

Between November 17 and December 11, 2025, four flagship models shipped in twenty-five days; in September 2026, four more shipped in four days. Each launch replays the same demonstrative grammar. Since July 2026, writers have begun reading the actual prompts behind the "one prompt" claims — and found engineering documents: sub-agent task allocation, a harsh critic agent, a loop instructed not to stop until "utterly perfect," and a compute bill that burned through 30% of a weekly quota. The cost of the spectacle is structurally hidden: the audience sees one sentence; the quota that burns belongs to someone else.

Spectacles are stratified by lifespan. ChatGPT itself (November 2022) sedimented into an everyday tool — in this essay's terms, it stopped being a spectacle and became infrastructure. The AlphaGo moment (2016) entered disciplinary memory. "One-prompt games" belong to the other class: they exist to be reposted, and once reposted they die. The "aha moment" in DeepSeek R1's training log is the same species — epiphany manufactured as a launch trope, retiring on the trope's schedule. **Spectacles have an ecology: the long-lived become ordinary, the short-lived live only to be shared** — and the rate of this stratification, this essay argues, is itself accelerating, and is measurable.

## 2. The spectacle as social relation

Debord's starting point is routinely misread. The spectacle is not "a collection of images" but a **social relation mediated by images**: direct experience recedes into representation, and encounters between people are organized through a media apparatus. AI launch culture is the latest annotation on this claim — launch streams, benchmark scorecards, KOL prompt-posts form a ritual system that repeatedly stages the question "is the machine intelligent?" Science-communication scholarship has already mapped this lineage: from the Turing Test through ImageNet competitions to ChatGPT's "realness tests," public verification has functioned as the ritual theater that legitimizes technology — Pfaffenberger's **technological dramas**, Latour's **theatre of proof**, grounded affectively in Nye's **technological sublime**.

Bareis et al.'s anatomy of the ChatGPT wave supplies the ritual's content analysis: four narrative pillars — strategic ignorance (the model staged as a "knowledge being"), consumption of the uncanny, the staged **battle** among tech giants, and eschatology (heaven and extinction sharing one microphone). They explicitly invoke Debord: audiences are not duped outsiders but **necessary components of the hype**, recruited through gazing and affective participation — no spectacle in an empty arena. *Conjuring algorithms* adds the magician's view: the industry deliberately invokes magical rhetoric to produce **dazzle**, deferring accountability past the point where mechanisms can be inspected.

## 3. The verbalization of fatigue

Repetition produced the antithesis. In 2026, "AI slop" spread from gaming complaints (over half of Steam Next Fest demos carrying AI disclosures) to a general diagnosis; industry posts announced peak "AI Demo Fatigue"; in the ACL 2025 retrospectives, Eduard Hovy's keynote label — "**LLM popcorn**," papers that fill you up and leave you empty — captured the majority genre in a conference where **67% of paper titles contained "LLM,"** and attendees wrote down "LLM exhaustion and nausea."

Fatigue has a **generational structure**. Douglas Adams's famous 1999 joke legislated three laws: everything present at your birth is normal; anything invented before you turn thirty is exciting and career-making; anything invented after thirty is against the natural order of things — "**until it's been around for about ten years, when it gradually turns out to be alright.**" That routinely-deleted final clause is the most important sentence in this essay: it prices the transformation from "against nature" to "taken for granted" at roughly a decade. The audience structure of spectacle is therefore generational: the same launch is revolution to one cohort and noise to another. Aesthetic fatigue is not an individual psychological event but a **misalignment of generational windows**. Adams also left the field its best definition — technology is "stuff that doesn't work yet": the endpoint of a spectacle is to become a chair.

## 4. The double temporality: utopia and anxiety

Two clocks tick inside the spectacle discourse. One is the clock of infinite futures: AGI's approach, capability extrapolation, civilizational promises — Altman's *The Intelligence Age* and Amodei's *Machines of Loving Grace*, for all their temperamental difference, share one rhetorical architecture: the self-exemption move, teleological naturalization, qualified acknowledgment of risk, and implicit indispensability. The other is the clock of quarterly reality: compute bills, IPO windows, subsidy shutdown dates. The function of the spectacle discourse is to keep the first clock occluding the second — *AGI Deep Hype* calls it **compressing time horizons to manufacture FOMO**; Bourne's analysis of affective capitalism identifies fear (of missing out, falling behind, losing power) as hype's most durable fuel. Reviewers, users, and investors are asked to simultaneously believe that "the spectacle is happening" and that "the spectacle must keep happening" — the second clause is the point.

## 5. Abbott's reminder

In his lectures on the future of knowledge production, Andrew Abbott offers a judgment worth quoting in full: our principal problems **antedate** the current technological revolution — "electronic wizardry is the **occasion of our disease, not its cause**." This supplies the line that separates this essay from popular narrative: spectacularization is not a new disease of the AI age but an old ailment of the prestige and attention economies, flaring up in a new host. Abbott's own method is equally instructive: begin from a minimal cultural artifact (a nineteenth-century ditty), exhaust its logical structure, and ascend to a sociological conclusion — while refusing every periodizing claim that "the digital revolution changed everything." Campolo's study of SOTA benchmark culture supplies the temporal frame: Hartog's **presentism**, a regime of time with only the "endless present" and no future; the SOTA ranking is that time institutionalized — it points at no future state and demands only the existence of the next ranking.

## 6. A research agenda

Restated as work: **(M1)** an event library of spectacles since 2022-11 — launches, demos, aha moments — paired with social-media mention curves to estimate half-lives and test acceleration; **(M2)** longitudinal coding of official essays, KOL posts, and fatigue discourse (is the rhetorical structure stable? is intensity inflating?); **(M3)** paired cases of long-lived versus ephemeral spectacles — under what conditions does a spectacle convert into everyday infrastructure. This essay states the problem; the data are left for the next round.
