# AI Blackouts: outages, the compensatory politics of resets, and the grey reseller tier

> On the morning of September 3, 2026, ChatGPT, Claude, and Grok became unreachable within the same window — three competing companies sharing one layer of cloud infrastructure, while Gemini survived on Google's own cloud. Downdetector logged over 340,000 reports, and the self-deprecating line that circulated on social media — "for a brief moment, millions of people had to use their brains again" — marked the turning point more precisely than any paper: **model access has become a utility, and utilities have blackouts**. Working from infrastructure studies, this essay handles three phenomena: how outages are experienced and narrated; how quota resets evolved into a compensatory politics; and how users in the grey reseller market live with services they know to be unstable.

## 1. Breakdown makes infrastructure visible

Of Susan Leigh Star and Karen Ruhleder's 1996 definition of infrastructure, the most-quoted clause is that infrastructure is **that which becomes visible upon breakdown**. The water-and-power analogy suddenly fits the AI age because it reactivates this old proposition: at the moment of unavailability, the ready-to-hand state of "AI folded into thinking" presents itself as a list of dependencies. The September 3 event supplied something better than theory: a natural control group. Three competitors falling together while Gemini survived completed, in one morning, the experiment researchers never got to run — **dissolving "competing products" back into "shared pipes."** The industry commentary collected by TechRound stated the conclusion plainly: "AI availability is next to cloud computing and electricity for the level of reliance we have." One commentator added the layer researchers tend to miss: the real casualty of an outage is not the person facing a blank chat box but the **agent mid-task** — its work does not pause gracefully; it halts, unrecorded.

## 2. Outage studies: metrology exists, meaning is open

Public LLM outages already have a metrology. An ICPE 2025 paper systematically characterized failures and recoveries across eight LLM services: strong weekday periodicity for OpenAI and Anthropic; same-day co-occurrence above 80% among services of the same provider; near-zero co-occurrence across providers. That last finding, published in early 2025, reads today as a rebuttal-in-advance of September 2026: the absence of co-occurrence was an *architectural fact at the time*, and infrastructural consolidation can rewrite it at will. The qualitative side holds exactly one preprint: a netnography of Reddit during the December 2024 ChatGPT outage, framed in attachment theory. In other words, **the metrology of failure has an owner; the sociology of failure is unclaimed** — particularly at the level of event sequences: within 2026, multi-hour downtimes became common enough that a single-vendor outage no longer constitutes news; what constitutes an event is correlated co-failure. That inflation of newsworthiness is itself a symptom of utility-ization. As for user narration, the viral quip deserves to be read as a headline: the blackout forced people to use their own brains again — its popularity indicates that outage is experienced as a **temporary repossession of cognitive outsourcing**, not merely service interruption.

## 3. The compensatory politics of resets

If outage is the moment of infrastructural failure, the quota reset is the institution's signature **compensatory ritual** — traditional utilities have no equivalent: the power company does not gift you a kilowatt-hour for a blackout. OpenAI's Codex made reset into a public art. On April 28, 2026, engineering lead Thibault Sottiaux ("Tibo") reset quotas for all paid plans because "it's been a good week"; on May 13, a compensatory reset after an incident; in early July, six resets in six days, two less than 72 hours apart; on June 11, resets became institutionalized as bankable "banked resets" with a referral scheme — invite a friend, both bank one, expiring in 30 days, a textbook loss-aversion design. Folk knowledge followed: knightli.com produced a four-fold taxonomy (incident compensation / launch promotion / growth milestone / competitive moat), and resetbeacon.com now exists to **forecast** the next goodwill reset — a vernacular forecasting station built for a compensatory ritual. Max Woolf's cynical reading supplies the fourth identity: frequent resets ensure that heavy users never get the chance to **try a competitor** once their quota runs dry. Compensation, promotion, milestone, moat — four identities stacked on one counter, with definitional authority residing in an engineer's tweet. An Anthropic-side headline summarized the theory: once an agent becomes a daily tool, **every interruption feels like machine downtime** — resets become outage insurance, underwritten by the party responsible for the outage.

## 4. The grey tier: living with instability

Where the official pipes do not reach — or cost too much — a large secondary market grew: reselling foreign model APIs to users who cannot pay in foreign currency or connect directly. Chinese-language investigations have opened this market: the NBD exposé ("1 yuan for 2.85 million tokens"), 36Kr's reconstruction of a station-master's grey career (¥2,000 startup cost; 60–70% of sites vanishing within three months of the regulatory squeeze), and a security team's audit of 25 shadow APIs (9 injecting malicious code, 17 harvesting cloud credentials, 1 stealing ether directly; substituted models averaging ~37% accuracy). This essay's interest is not exposure — the journalists finished that — but an unresearched question: **how do users live long-term with services they know to be unstable?** The reporting already yields a repertoire of coping techniques: multi-site redundancy (subscribing to two or three relays against vanishings), small top-ups (bounding loss as a "cheap lesson"), and a folk theory of "model dumbing-down" (users claiming to detect substitution by feel). Why do they support it? Price, geo-blocking, RMB payment, and a kind of sovereign claim on instability — the unreliable experience itself is the price of bargaining power. The theory for this position already exists: Simone's "**people as infrastructure**" and Anand's *Hydraulic City* both describe an everyday in which reliability is not provided elsewhere but woven, by people, out of instability itself.

## 5. Coda: two-tier infrastructure and a temporal contract

Stacked together, the three phenomena describe a two-tier structure: the official pipes run on earnings-season time, the grey tier on vanishing time, and users flow between them. The split between premium and standard networks that Graham and Marvin described for urban infrastructure has its AI edition. The preliminary proposition of this essay: AI's utility-ization produces not a more reliable infrastructure but a new **temporal contract** — users are asked to accept "may interrupt at any time, may be compensated at any time, may be locked in at any time" as normal; instability is becoming a structural ingredient of the business rather than an accident. A fuller research plan (event library, reset archive, reseller-community observation) is registered separately.
