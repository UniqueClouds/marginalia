> An unflattering but serious analogy: journals like Nature and Science, and Chinese top-tier AI WeChat accounts like QbitAI and Synced, are becoming the same institution at different purities. The basis is not form (the former has peer review, the latter does not) but logic: **top journals are letting their editorial taste serve two non-academic forces — the pace of iterating technology, and the preferences of their imagined audience.** This invisible taste orientation bends the topics journals accept, and submissions bend in turn; the redirection is non-technical, cultural and commercial-consumptive in kind. This essay maps its academic coordinates, its empirical base, and an operable research design.

## 1. The analogy's reverse evidence

Start from a Chinese joke: Synced, QbitAI, and 新智元 are nicknamed the "**Chinese AI Big Three conferences**." It is sarcasm — mocking their clickbait titles ("physics has ceased to exist"), their priced soft-placements, their practice of publicizing papers before acceptance — but the structure of the sarcasm matters: **calling influencers "conferences" concedes that in public perception, journals and traffic media are already doing the same thing.** The English counterpart is Eduard Hovy's ACL 2025 keynote term "**LLM popcorn**" — most papers are popcorn, filling and then empty — in a conference where 67% of titles contained "LLM," and retrospectives wrote down "LLM exhaustion and nausea." The Chinese side offers a more concrete object: platforms like Tencent News run a standing genre of "Westlake University completes the CNS grand slam in four days" and "Tsinghua-Peking CNS league tables" — top-journal publications rendered as collegiate sports standings, counted weekly, narrated as hat-tricks. **At its extreme, the mediatization of science gives research a league narrative.**

## 2. Mediatization: a ready theoretical anchor

The analogy has a ready anchor in science-communication research: the **medialization (mediatization) of science**. Since 1998 Weingart has argued that the coupling of science and media systems orients research organizations toward media — staged media events, results publicized before peer review, journals selling themselves to the press; his 2022 revisit sharpens the mechanism: institutional communication takes its rationality from "**imagined publics**," and "public engagement" has derailed into marketing, branding, and PR. Väliverronen adds the middle-range concept of **promotional culture**. The crucial step belongs to Moorhead et al., who proposed in a qualitative study that **journals themselves are mediatized** — scientists bend toward journals' norms more than toward journalists'; a participant: "if your paper wasn't in Science or Nature — you did not tell the press department." Journals are not victims dragged by media; they are an active link in the mediatization chain.

## 3. The empirical base of taste redirection

That top-journal taste is drifting is not an impression; it is measured, and the measurements show **positive feedback**. *Communications Psychology* (2025) analyzed 130,000 PNAS/Science/Nature abstracts from 1991–2023: each ~1% increase in promotional language (about two words) predicts 9–14% more yearly citations and a 3–6% higher Altmetric score — **hyping pays, directly into the metrics academia cares about**. Longitudinally, positive words in PubMed abstracts rose from 2.0% to 17.5% over four decades (+880%), with "robust," "novel," and "unprecedented" up to 150-fold in relative frequency (Vinkers et al., BMJ 2015). The subtlest result is Sumner's pair of studies: the main source of exaggerated science news is universities' and journals' own press releases (OR 6.5–56), and **exaggeration does not buy more coverage** — the taste misalignment is "adaptive but ineffective": the whole system inflates for a reward that does not exist. The funding layer has the same structure: a 2026 China–US comparison shows AI grant abstracts saturated with macro-narrative language (in Chinese contexts: industrial empowerment, chokepoint challenges, major national needs) relative to cancer immunology — language that vanishes at publication, a "strategic textual decoupling" imposing a hidden cognitive tax.

## 4. Research design: the three-tier transcode chain

Turning the analogy into research, the core device is a **three-tier transcode chain**: for the same paper, from journal press release to international media coverage to Chinese-language self-media, measure the hype gain and deformation at each step. A methodological template exists: Leidecker-Sandmann et al. used Altmetric's mainstream-media score (MSM≥100) to identify papers with intense international coverage, then examined post-coverage growth of thematically similar PubMed publications — the "media–journal topic convergence" test migrates directly into an "LLM topic redirection" test. Combined with Sumner-style tiered content coding and a 130k-abstract panel, three questions follow: the rate of redirection (LLM-ization of top-journal topic distributions, benchmarked against non-AI hotspot eras); the transmission mechanism (how editorials, News & Views selection, and covers encode the imagined audience); and the entirely unresearched Chinese side (CNS-league sportification and the "Big Three conferences" discourse have zero academic treatment).

## 5. Naming and positioning

For a name: the scholarly canon is **mediatization**; media studies offers **tabloidization** (a precise description of taste bending to mass preference); moving toward field theory, Bourdieu's answer is the victory of the **heteronomous pole** (heteronomization): when journals' taste serves an imagined audience, the scientific field slides from its autonomous pole — the line connects directly to note 009 on this site; and if one wants a coinage that catches Hovy's phrase, **popcornization** is available. One boundary must be declared: the analogy's claim is **logical convergence, not institutional identity** — top journals have peer review and community accountability, influencers do not; conflating them would abolish the very thing the research needs to explain.
